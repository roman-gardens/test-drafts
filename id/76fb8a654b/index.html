Province Description (Asia)

The region of what is now modern Turkey became a part of the Roman Empire in 133 BCE when king Attalos III of Pergamum bequeathed his kingdom to the Roman people. The province included the heartland of the Pergamene kingdom as well as the territories of Mysia, Lydia, Ionia, Caria, western Phrygia and the eastern Aegean islands. Under Augustus in 27 BCE, Asia was declared a senatorial province and the governance of a proconsul. The capital of the province was Ephesos, although Pergamon may have fulfilled this role initially. Under Diocletian (284-305 CE) the province was subdivided into smaller administrative units: Hellespontus, Asia, Lydia, Caria, Phrygia I and Phrygia II. Due to its excellent harbors on the west coast and its many navigable rivers, Asia's water-borne trade with the rest of the Mediterranean flourished. In the second century CE, Asia experienced great prosperity, to which the growth and architectural aggrandizement of many of its cities attest. In the area of Kibyra, bordering the regions of Lycia, Caria, Phrygia and Pisidia, numerous inscriptions refer to the large imperial estates of Roman families. Woolen textiles, marble, and timber constituted a significant part of the commodities exported from the province.

While the Romans might have looked to the Greeks for inspiration in philosophy, architecture, sculpture, and the fine arts, the non-Greek peoples of the eastern Mediterranean are their inspiration for luxury, fine-living, and the status conferred by displays of individual wealth (Athenaeus Deip. XV.690). Asia was a center for such splendor, first in the kingdoms of Lydia, Phrygia, and Lycia, and then continued by the Persian satraps, and later the Hellenistic kings. The Persian paradeisos is particularly well-attested in literature referencing Asia. That these gardens are known primarily from Roman-period literary sources indicates their continuing influence. For example, long after the gold of Lydia's Pactolus River had disappeared, Lydia remained a literary topos, not only for copious wealth, but for a life of luxury set in a landscape of natural abundance. Athenaeus (Deipnosophistae 12.515d-f) wrote in the second century CE that

"The Lydians in their luxury planned parks and made them like gardens and so kept in the shade. For they thought it more dainty not to have the sun's rays touch them at all..."

Other districts of Asia captured the Roman imagination: Pliny the Elder writes of an ancient plane tree (Sycamorus orientalis) in Lycia with a girth of sufficient circumference to house a grotto in its hollow trunk, ornamented with pumice and moss with provisions for 18 people to dine there (Pliny HN 12.9).

The paradeisoi of the Persian satraps are of particular influence on the introduction of horti and gardens of Rome. The paradeisos, from the Persian, paira daeza, or "enclosed place," is a term that had evolved by the Hellenistic period to mean a large enclosed park with luxurious gardens as well as orchards, collections of trees, crops, and hunting areas (see Sardis below). It was at Sardis that the Greek Lysander visited Cyrus the Younger, a visit recorded by Clearchus and retold by Xenophon. It is an essential moment in garden history recounted for the Romans by Cicero, De Senectute. 17.59. These paradeisoi, in the sense of luxurious orchards, are said by the Romans to have been brought to Greece by Kimon (Aristot. apud Plut. Cim. 10; comp. Cic. de Off. 2.18Plutarch), and were adopted by Hellenistic kings throughout Greece and southern Italy during the late Hellenistic period. The hunting parks at Daskyleion and Celenae are also vividly described by Xenophon (See Anabasis 1.2.7, 2.4.14 and Oeconomicus 4.13). Xenophon also describes how paradeisoi were occupied or cut down by armies during the fourth and third centuries BCE. Appreciation for these parks apparently continued, and Seleucus of Syria took over the royal paradeisos at Sardis (Plutarch, Demetrios 50.6). Years of instability followed, so it is difficult to know what the Romans themselves thought of paradeisoi in Asia in the second and first centuries BCE. Notable examples of paradeisoi, in some form, may have been visible to Romans visiting Asia, as well. For example, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus is now thought by its excavators to have been set in a grove or park.

Thus, the paradeisos, the alsos, the gymnasium, and the quadriporticus are not forms the Romans suddenly "discovered." They had had a long influence and were integrated into the Hellenistic architectural forms of the Italic landscape. In the second century BCE, however, when the Roman generals began to define themselves as rulers of an empire, they turned directly to the palaces and paradeisoi of the Hellenistic monarchs to establish their power, to display spoils of war, and to create the luxurious setting for life in Rome. They further adapted the evolving quadriporticus form for display to the public.

Despite the importance of Asia Minor in the history of the Roman landscape, archaeological evidence is scant. The excavators contacted for this essay indicated that parks, gardens, and other planted areas have yet to become a subject of research design for the region and finds appear to be few—primarily inscriptions and wall paintings. Most of the smaller domestic courtyards—often the most manageable to investigate - are paved. The larger palaestrae, gymnasia, and enclosed open spaces have not been investigated for landscape architectural and planting features, although recently an important campaign of work in the South Agora at Aphrodisias is setting an important precedent (see below). This catalog includes areas with dirt and incomplete paved surfaces, and notes sites where the excavators have some level of optimism about fruitful future study. The subject of designed landscapes, gardens, and planted areas in Asia Minor awaits archaeological investigation.

Place Description (Sardis)

Sardis was the capital of Lydia. With wealth from gold washed down in the Pactolus River, the Lydians held a reputation for luxury long before the Persian conquest. Around 395-394 BCE, the Spartan general Aegilaus overran the paradeisos of the Persian king Tissaphernes, a park that “had been set out artfully and expensively with plants and all else that contributes to luxury and the quiet enjoyment of the good things.” Between Sardis and Thybarnae, he stationed 1400 soldiers in a densely wooded place to ambush the Persians (Diodorus 14.80.2). While we have no descriptions, per se, of gardens in the royal palace, the landscape of Sardis lying at the foot of the flowery and vine-clad Mt. Tmolus was a literary topos for fertility and fine-living.

The paradeisos of Cyrus was not destroyed, but rather admired, by another Spartan, the general Lysander (Xenophon, Oeconomicus, 4.20-25). This incident is also noted by Cicero: “Lysander, a man of great capacity, had brought [Cyrus the Younger] gifts from the allies, so that Cyrus was in many ways kindly and generous toward him and also showed him a certain carefully arranged pleasure-park” (De senectute 59). One satrap transplanted Syrian trees and plants to Lydia. On the death of Alexander, the royal paradeisoi in Sardis were taken over by Seleucus of Syria (Plutarch, Demetrios 50.6).

A late fourth-century BCE inscription recovered from Sardis mentions a paradeisos with a distinctly agrarian meaning. “Outside the farmsteads are two paradeisoi requiring 15 artabas of seeds.” Either the term had become very general by this period, or the inscription refers to the practice of combination-cropping between trees (Columella 9.12 ). Buckler and Robinson suggest an interpretation of paradeisos as “land not only with fruit trees or orchards but with gardens of flowers and vegetables and perhaps also grain.”

Garden Description

The Bath/Gymnasium Complex at Sardis is one of the features that the excavators suggest could have been planted, though no evidence for tree pits and planting beds has been discerned to date. The whole palaestra was cleared of 40-60 cm of overburden down to the level of the original surface. From there, only limited trenches have been made into the subsurface.

The large palaestra was laid out in the form of a square peristyle court, 100 columns on each side, in the Corinthian order. The columns were 6.97 m. high. Although the area of the palaestra was clearly planned from the outset, the colonnade itself was constructed in the third century CE. It was entered through a triple gate on the east, where the bath was. A synagogue on the south side did not open into the peristyle. The palaestra sat on a built-up terrace, resting in part on vaulted substructures that have not been fully explored archaeologically. A long vaulted drain runs north-south under the palaestra and synagogue. The terrace appears to have been constructed in the first century CE, while the palaestra itself was developed in the third century CE.

The surface of the palaestra is graded gently from east to west. A covered drain outside the stylobate directed rain water from the roof through perforated drain covers in the NW and SE corners. Here water was directed into the N-S vaulted drain. Manhole covers in the synagogue and in the central open space of the palaestra access this large drain.

Archaeologists found limestone slab paving around the edges of the palaestra court. In the latest phases of the complex the palaestra was paved in limestone or marble slabs in east-west rows set into a mortar of crushed brick, which retains the impression of missing slabs. The reports indicate that the paving and mortar was only preserved along the edges, but their interpretation is that the entire surface was paved. In the center of the Palaestra a linear or cruciform area of sub-paving was discovered, raised some 10-15 cm higher than the surrounding level. The E-W expanse of this “road” 5.5 m in width extends between the Palaestra east gate and the center bays of the screen colonnade on the west on the central axis. F. Yegul does not describe it as a cruciform structure, nor does C. Greenewalt. Yegul dates this feature to the second half of the fifth century, possibly as late as the sixth c. CE.

The palaestra offers interesting directions for further study. The present subsurface levels may indicate through coring or trenches whether earlier surfaces were of compacted lenses of dirt or sand for paths or were loose, tilled and amended soils characteristic of cultivation. The presence of paving around the edges of the palaestra may nonetheless allow for tree pits or other features towards the center. The late cruciform feature perhaps indicates division of the palaestra for such a purpose. No cross-plan path designs have yet been attested for gardens or palaestra of the Roman era, though the cruciform pool at Herculaneam is striking. The late date of this feature suggests early evidence for a cross-plan, or charharbagh design, of the Byzantine period.

A short section of drain was also found in the NE quadrant of the courtyard. The archaeologists suggest that the overall paving of the palaestra’s courtyard may be late, after the use of the peristyle as an exercise ground had ended. Greenewalt has suggested that the palaestra was surfaced in sand and gravel for the earlier function. Further investigation of the paving mortar and the subsurface layers may provide indication for planting of trees to shade the large area during its various phases of use.
