Province Description (Italia)

Italia was not considered a province until the reign of Diocletian. However, according to Pliny's Naturalis Historia, the peninsula was divided into eleven administrative regions by Augustus around 7 BCE:

  • Regio I: Latium et Campania
    • Includes major sites in Roma, Pompeii, and Herculaneum
  • Regio II: Apulia et Calabria
  • Regio III: Lucania et Bruttium
  • Regio IV: Samnium
  • Regio V: Picenum
  • Regio VI: Umbria et Ager Gallicus
  • Regio VII: Etruria
  • Regio VIII: Aemilia
  • Regio IX: Liguria
  • Regio X: Venetia et Histria
  • Regio XI: Transpadana

Place Description (Latium et Campania)

Around 7 BCE, Latium et Campania were defined as Regio I of Italia by Augustus. This region includes major archaeological sites in Roma, Pompeii, and Herculaneum.

Garden Description

The precise etymology of its name is not well documented, as the nineteenth-century excavation reports are very brief. However, it is possible that the fact that it was one of the largest houses in Herculaneum, with a splendid view of the gulf, led people at the time of the first explorations (between 1835 and 1855) to mistakenly believe that the building was a hotel. The peristyle comprises a portico supported by nine by eleven columns. Following construction, a low wall in opus reticulatum was erected between the columns. The substantial green space, situated within the portico, is accessible via a small masonry staircase located in the central part of the southern intercolumniation. The portico is raised by eight steps (1.10 m) above the garden's ground level. The structure is equipped with a drainage channel that runs along the perimeter, one step below the portico. This channel directs rainwater towards a cistern situated beneath the two wells. The wells' openings are strategically positioned between the columns, at the centre of the eastern and western sides. Each corner featured a shallow square basin, specifically designed for the purification of domestic water. During the initial explorations, the layout of the garden (set within the portico) led to speculation that it might have been a poultry yard. During the excavations in the late 1920s, the discovery of a charred pear tree trunk (Pyrus communis L.) indicated that the area might have been a pomarius (orchard).
