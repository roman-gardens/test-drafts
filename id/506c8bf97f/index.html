Province Description (Libya)

Place Description (Leptis Magna)

The villas of Silin are part of a group of seaside residences built on the coast by the rich ship owners of Leptis Magna. At the beginning of the second century CE, the Roman rulers, trying to make a better landing place, succeeded only in silting up Leptis' harbor. Thus the Leptitans were forced to go out along the coast. There they created their own ports beside which they erected spacious and lavish residences built on high promontories, so that they could oversee their trades and manage the large estates extending behind their houses. For fifteen miles on both sides of Leptis Magna every marsa (as sandy beaches are called in Libya) had its landing place, and each promontory its villa. Goodchild set them on his map of the area. Salza Prina Ricotti studied two of these residences naming them Silin's villas, and Luisa Musso examined a third one, with results published for the first time.

Garden Description

The Villa of the Little Circus rose on a high promontory two kilometers to the east of the Maritime Odeon. It overlooked the end of a long, sandy and curving beach. This once imposing residence had a 300 meter front on the sea. At the rear of the building, an area free of masonry was probably occupied by ornamental bushes and trees [A], while in the eastern part of the villa, a square peristyle, with the remains of a basin necessary for irrigation, was certainly a garden [B] and so must have been also the nearby central courtyard [C].

However the most interesting garden of this villa was located on the extreme western and higher part of the promontory. Here a long, narrow enclosure [D], shaped like a circus, occupied an area 85 meters long and 15 meters wide. Two walls enclosed its length. In its center we can still see the remains of a flowerbed, 60 meters long and 6 meters wide, shaped like the spina of a circus. Two semicircular basins of water, set at each end, stood at the places which, in a real circus, would have been occupied by the metae. A square basin [d] divided the flowerbed into two unequal parts. Viewed from a tower triclinium which stood at the western end [E], this inequality created the optical illusion of a much longer garden than the space on the top of the cliff would permit, as shown in Fig. 5.

Gardens shaped like a circus, a stadium or a hippodrome came into fashion around the end of the first century CE (Pliny. Epist. 5.6.32-40). The list of other gardens of this includes the hippodrome of Pliny the Younger's Tuscany villa; the stadium in Domitian's Domus Flavia on the Palatine hill; the one in this emperor's country residence of villa Albana (Lugli 1918); and the so-called Stadium of Hadrian's Villa (Hoffman 1980).

For the other villas of the Libyan coast and representations of country life on mosaics see: Mariani 1914; Mariani 1914a; Mariani 1914b; Romanelli 1922; Bartoccini 1927a; Bartoccini 1928-29; Cagiano de Azevedo 1962
