Cilicia

Place Description

The area of Cilicia along the southern coast of Anatolia, pivotal to maritime routes linking ancient Asia Minor with the kingdoms and provinces of the east, continuously shifted under Roman control from the first century B.C. until the reign of Diocletian. The region was historically divided into the mountainous ‘Rough Cilicia’ (Cilicia Tracheia) to the west, and ‘Plain of Cilicia’ (Cilicia Pedias), along the eastern coast, which famously became associated with pirates exploiting maritime trade in the eastern Mediterranean. The Tarsus Mountains formed its northern border, with the Anatolian plateau to the west, the Syrian desert plains to the east, and the Mediterranean to the south. In 102 B.C., following the breakdown of the Seleucid Empire, part of the area was established as a province of Rome. It was not until after Pompey had defeated the last of the pirates in 67 B.C., and its inhabitants were subjugated, that Cilicia was settled permanently. Parts of the territory were alternately dissolved, granted to vassal kings, or governed as part of Syria until 72 A.D., when Cilicia became a province again under the reign of Vespasian, with a capital at Tarsus and with close administrative connection to the island of Cyprus. At the end of the third century A.D. Diocletian incorporated ‘Rough Cilicia’ as its own province, with a new title of Isauria, and a capital at Seleucia.

While the mountainous region of ‘Rough Cilicia’ offered less cultivable land to its inhabitants, the fertile plains to the east were well-watered and provided adequate conditions for agricultural exploitation, including the production of flax, vines, and olives. Columella (De Ag. 2.10.18) notes that sesame was grown in Cilicia during the summer months. Other ancient literary accounts boast of the sweet wine of Cilicia (Pliny HN 14.81) and its highly desired fruit trees. The region produced some of the more well-known saffron of the Roman world (Martial 8.14), particularly the Corcyrian crocus, named after a spring-fed cavern of Corcyra near the coast. Yet aside from these textual references, there has been no garden archaeology performed within the region’s numerous towns and estates. New projects such as the Rough Cilicia Regional Archaeological Survey Project, which incorporate pollen studies, dendrochronology, and pedestrian survey, suggest new bodies of evidence for better understanding ancient cultivation and garden culture in the area.
